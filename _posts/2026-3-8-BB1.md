I’ve heard good stories from BridgeBio (BB) recently and thought about it might be interesting to see how its capital structure has been developing since the IPO in 2019. It is a typical fast-growing biotech that successfully managing an IPO, securing multi-tranche debt, and protecting shareholder equity while burning cash for late-stage clinical trials. After all, the survival of a biotech requires more than just credible science, but also a "fortress" financial strategy.

**2019: The IPO and the "Fortress" Balance Sheet**

Founded in 2015 to target Mendelian diseases and genetic cancers, BridgeBio entered 2019 with subsidiaries and licensing deals (notably with Novartis). Its primary asset, acoramidis (BBP-265), sat within its subsidiary Eidos, which was already publicly traded.

In 2019, several factors reshaped the company's capital structure:

The IPO significantly increased equity, providing over $360M in cash.

An additional tranche of the term loan was added, resulting in BridgeBio (BB) holding $75M in debt due in 2023. While the lender did not restrict the company’s decisions regarding disposals, investments, or acquisitions, this flexibility came with a strict covenant: BB must make a mandatory prepayment equal to 75% of net cash proceeds received from the sale or licensing of any pledged assets, such as intellectual property or subsidiaries.

Its primary subsidiary, Eidos, secured a three-tranche agreement with Silicon Valley Bank and Hercules Capital (it will be interesting to see how the SVB bankruptcy eventually impacts BB’s position). Eidos ultimately drew $17.5M from Tranche A in 2019.
Eidos also issued 24M shares to the public market.

By the end of 2019, BB held a substantial amount of cash from both the IPO and new loans. Beyond operating expenses, which consumed over 30% of cash flow, BB reserved approximately 44% of its cash on hand for liquidity and emergencies. The remaining 26% was invested in marketable securities—ranging from money market funds to commercial paper and corporate notes. This was a sound approach, as these liquid securities provided a safe way to earn modest returns while extending the company's "cash runway."

A 2019 debt-to-equity ratio of 0.19 is considered healthy by almost any industry standard, though perhaps not yet the "optimal" mix for a scaling firm. Despite this stability, questions remained regarding the sustainability of widening operating losses due to expensive R&D and the potential for further dilution from future funding rounds.2020: Navigating the COVID-19 Liquidity Crunch

**2020: Navigating the COVID-19 Liquidity Crunch**

2020 brought the dual challenge of COVID-19 and high-burn clinical milestones. Trials were delayed, and the path to FDA approval grew uncertain. To ensure survival, BB executed a massive $550M private placement of 2.50% Senior Convertible Notes due 2027. While investors decide when to convert, BB retains the right to settle those conversions in cash, stock, or a combination of both. This half-billion-dollar injection proved essential, as 2020 operating cash flow requirements would have otherwise exhausted cash on hand.

Convertible bonds naturally carry the risk of future share dilution. For a company like BB, with several Phase 3 products in the pipeline, the potential for a soaring stock price is high. Consequently, existing shareholders faced a strong likelihood of dilution, and BB risked having to pay a value significantly higher than the original principal upon conversion.

To mitigate these risks, BB allocated a portion of the 2027 Note proceeds to two strategic maneuvers: $50M for capped call transactions and a $75M stock repurchase.

**Capped Calls**: These function as call options that cover the cost of extra shares if the bonds are significantly "in the money." They effectively "cancel out" dilution because the counterparty bank delivers the "extra" shares to BB, which then passes them to bondholders. For a high-growth company, this $50M serves as relatively inexpensive long-term insurance against ownership dilution.

**Stock Repurchase**: Why reduce equity when the debt-to-equity ratio is already climbing? The answer lies in "convertible arbitrage." Many institutional noteholders, particularly hedge funds, simultaneously short-sell the company’s stock to achieve a "market neutral" position. While these funds profit from coupon payments and short rebates, their large-scale shorting can cause the stock price to plummet—harming shareholders and complicating future debt pricing. BB's repurchase created immediate demand to counter this downward pressure. Furthermore, the repurchase helped secure a higher initial conversion price for the 2027 Notes, putting BB in a stronger position for future settlements.

By the end of 2020, BB had significantly reduced its investments in marketable securities to prioritize operational liquidity and these financing strategies. Approximately 44% of the year's total cash remained liquid—consistent with 2019 levels—even as the overall debt-to-equity ratio skyrocketed to 4.0. Investors did not necessarily view this as a red flag, given the unique uncertainties of the pandemic and the nature of high-growth biotech. Nevertheless, BB’s management faced the formidable challenge of meeting creditor obligations and maintaining operations while navigating a drastically altered macro environment.
