It's not often we see central governments team up to "save" a currency. On July 31, the US intervened directly in the yen market — selling euros from its reserves to buy yen. The effect was immediate: the yen jumped from roughly 163 per dollar to around 157. The last time we saw anything comparable was back in 2011, in the aftermath of the Fukushima earthquake, though that intervention had a completely different objective.

![YenFX](/assets/images/YenFX.png)

**Why did the US do this?**

The yen has been depreciating for years, and Japan's economy has been stuck in a prolonged negative cycle as a result. That slide recently pushed the currency to a fresh 40-year low, trading near 164 per dollar. At the same time, Japan is too important to the US — both politically and economically — to sit on the sidelines. It's the largest foreign holder of US Treasury bonds, with $1.14 trillion on hand, roughly 13% of all foreign-held US federal debt.

A weaker yen raises the risk that Japan could start unwinding that position, triggering a broader debt sell-off. If that happens, bond prices fall — and as prices fall, yields and interest rates climb. That translates into higher borrowing costs, pricier loans and corporate debt, and added pressure on equity markets.
